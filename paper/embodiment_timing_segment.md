## Embodiment design, development timing and the customer opportunity window (working paper segment)

Robotic capability improvement need not occur on fixed physical capital. A configuration change may lower a persistent service error, improve adaptation productivity or increase accepted output per machine. It may also postpone the first date of service. Thus a comparison of two policies on the same hardware is not enough to rank investments in new embodiments. Conversely, faster development does not guarantee a valuable deployment if additional output cannot be sold or the customer already has a superior alternative.

We study a tractable post-deployment investment menu. A provider with finite cash can expand the established service, adapt an existing configuration to a second market, redesign and then qualify a second configuration, or retain liquidity. The old and redesigned configurations face an identical conservative whole-service criterion. Their adaptation cost is generated by a component-level cost-minimization program; output is capped by qualified configuration capacity; and a customer's chosen production responds to final-demand economics. The provider receives a specified share of incremental operating surplus after the customer outside option. This is a single-provider partial-equilibrium model, not a claim about economy-wide market structure.

The redesigned embodiment reduces a synthetic irreducible failure floor and raises adaptation effectiveness, enabling more qualified output per unit, but incurs an additional development lag. If the customer-revenue intercept declines over the investment horizon, redesign's additional capacity may not arrive while its output is sufficiently valuable. In the full-capacity region, the redesign-minus-adaptation value difference is affine in demand erosion, with a slope determined by capacity-weighted exposure to future periods. A four-window exact example yields three optimal responses as demand erosion rises: redesign, quicker adaptation, then further incumbent capacity. The results are conditional and depend on specified demand, cost, qualification and contractual institutions; their numerical values are not industry estimates.

The existence of a crossing is not itself a new contribution. Time-to-build and optimal capacity choice are existing economic topics (e.g. Jeon, 2023). The candidate empirical object is the *joined* source-to-target transition: fixed service acceptance standard, hardware or policy adjustment inputs, calendar qualification delay, accepted customer production and the corresponding receipts/finance. Robotic-hand research such as LEAP Hand illustrates the importance of mechatronic design and experimental cost tradeoffs, but does not supply this industrial cost-and-demand panel. Accordingly, this passage is an analytical benchmark within the research program, not a settled main theorem.

The next empirical step should locate a documented production task and its best feasible incumbent alternative, then compare configurations on matched service requirements. The production-network atlas must preserve sector-level monetary flows separately from task-level technical and commercial relationships.
