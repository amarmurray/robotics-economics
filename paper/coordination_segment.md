## Finite human attention and the economic scale of embodied production

Digital coordination and physical robots need not be interchangeable productive capital. In a representative finite-source fleet model, each active robot generates blocking exceptions with rate λ and human supervisors clear those exceptions with service rate μ. Let N be installed robots, c supervisors and k interrupted robots. Their transitions follow `k→k+1` at `(N-k)λ` and `k→k-1` at `min(k,c)μ`. The stationary distribution gives output-equivalent capacity `K=r(N-E[k])` for robot productive rate r. Flow balance implies `λ E[N-k] = μ E[min(k,c)] <= cμ`, hence `K <= rcμ/λ`, with equality approached at large N and fixed λ>0, μ and c. This is a standard finite-source repairman constraint, not an independent theorem about robot firms.

In this economy, installing another robot may principally increase the number awaiting scarce intervention. Software that reduces blocking requests can increase effective capacity across the **existing** fleet, whereas purchasing another supervisor increases the number of simultaneous recoveries. The allocation depends on investment cost, funding, and the customer's willingness to purchase additional *accepted output*. Under the same quadratic customer revenue used elsewhere in this paper, marginal customer value falls as output rises. Thus hardware and digital coordination may be **technical complements** (a software improvement increases the marginal productive output from another robot) while becoming **economic substitutes** in a relatively shallow demand market. A nonnegative technical cross-capacity `K_HD-K_D-K_H+K_0` does not establish that the analogous private-value cross-difference is nonnegative.

For an adopted and capacity-bound customer earning `G(K)=(a-v)K-bK²/2`, and a two-part contract assigning share ρ of incremental gross operating profit over the best feasible alternative to the provider, the cross-difference of private operating profit is `ρ[(a-v) C_K - (b/2)(K_HD²-K_D²-K_H²+K_0²)]`. For `C_K>0`, its sign turns at `(a-v)*=(b/2)(K_HD²-K_D²-K_H²+K_0²)/C_K` if all four demand/adoption regimes remain interior to capacity. This result makes a falsifiable distinction between the geometry of productive complements and the economics of their deployment. The condition can disappear when demand saturates, the contractual capture rate changes, exception classes are nonblocking or shared support costs are nonlinear.

A rational-arithmetic illustration with three units and one supervisor has a strictly positive physical cross-effect (`961508/2917887` units/time), while the private cross-effect changes sign as the customer demand intercept changes—negative when `a-v=23/4`, positive when `a-v=8`. In a four-unit investment comparison, adding hardware is best with infrequent blocking events; digital intervention reduction is best with frequent blocking events when cash can fund only one improvement; the two together can be best when enough cash is available. These synthetic comparisons are not empirical cost or utilization estimates.

The need to model human attention is evidenced by Serve Robotics' 2025 Form 10-K description of remote video supervisors and occasional field interventions in sidewalk delivery, and by Agility Robotics' different descriptions of cloud fleet management, remote diagnostics and autonomous Digit workcells. A third research mechanism is ThriftyDAgger's intervention-budget gate. None of these sources measures the intervention arrival and service distributions in the synthetic example. A credible empirical version requires matched robot-active exposure, blocked versus nonblocking exception duration, human availability, complete accepted output, contracts, and actual payable coordination costs. The key question is therefore not how many robots one person *can supervise* in principle but **how the economically valuable, safety-compliant attention requirement evolves with the qualified fleet and its tasks**.
