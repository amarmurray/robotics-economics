## Manufacturing experience, hardware specialization and heterogeneous reinvestment

The downstream provider's deployment portfolio responds not only to experience with executing robotic tasks, but also to the manufacturing knowledge generated by prior builds and to the legal terms under which supplier cost improvements reach the purchaser. Operating experience may improve control policies or verification effort, while build experience may reduce production resource requirements. Neither automatically reduces the enterprise's dated purchasing obligation.

Suppose a provider chooses between one generalist unit and a specialist delivering greater accepted output in the same target customer service. If target demand is binding, a specialist's extra customer-output surplus depends on the customer demand intercept, the nonlinear final-demand margin, the provider's captured share and the specialist qualification investment. An input-cost reduction that favors the specialist changes the entry threshold only to the extent it changes the *invoice* the provider must pay. If the supplier retains the savings, the same real learning can have a smaller private effect on the deployer. The exact expression and its regime restrictions are proved in [Q5](../research/manufacturing_2026_11_17/RESEARCH_NOTE.md).

A second, finite capital-allocation example shows why cash eligibility and economic attractiveness must be distinguished: making two initially low-return generalist robots creates manufacturing experience that lowers a future specialized purchase price. In the stated synthetic case the supplier pass-through share required to fund target entry is 11/20, but the higher share required to make that sequence privately preferable to direct entry is 3/5. A sufficiently valuable target customer and a cross-embodiment input transfer are necessary, while more manufacturing experience without invoice savings may be privately irrelevant. The example does not establish that physical assembly experience transfers to the particular new morphology in real facilities.

This complements the earlier embodiment timing, task-data transfer, coordination and pricing results. It also highlights the unit-of-observation problem for empirics: aggregate industrial-robot installations and announced nameplate factory capacity are not realized within-generation builds, yields or supplier prices. Public Apptronik–Jabil and Agility announcements inform the organizational mapping, but identifying the slopes requires repeated production cohorts and matched supplier-purchaser invoices. Generic contract-manufacturer learning and capacity timing remain prior art, so a research paper must isolate what the joined production/service/cash network adds rather than label all learning an original idea.
