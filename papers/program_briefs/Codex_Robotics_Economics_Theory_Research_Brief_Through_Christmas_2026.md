## 3. The intellectual architecture

### 3.1 Organizing level

Retain the hierarchy:

`end market × value-chain stage × operating archetype × customer organization × geography × time`.

Tasks and service bundles explain what happens inside those cells. Do not collapse the program back to a warehouse task or equate occupation counts with automatically addressable robot demand.

For candidate deployment, distinguish task capability, qualified complete service, commercial access, profitable economics, financeability, supported scale, and actual adoption. These are related but different objects.

### 3.2 A multilayer and multipayer production network

Represent connected layers with explicit units and economic meanings:

- Material/service production: inputs, operations, outputs, handoffs, capacities, complementarities, and downstream demand.
- Capabilities and embodiments: which combinations of hardware, software, environmental preparation, human work, and integration satisfy a service requirement.
- Data and learning: directed transfer between source and target configuration–task–environment combinations, with rights, processing, adaptation, and validation requirements.
- Information/control/coordination: scheduling, supervision, maintenance, exception handling, authority, latency, and shared resources.
- Ownership/contracts: asset title, decision rights, operating authority, support obligations, data access, and the ability to reorganize work.
- Cash/finance: each payer's collections, operating outlays, capital investments, receivables, financing, and obligations.

Use a graph, multilayer graph, or hypergraph as justified. A service requiring several complementary capabilities may be a joint constraint/hyperedge, not a collection of independently substitutable pairwise links. The representation should reveal mechanisms, not add mathematical decoration.

Distinguish a changing state on a fixed topology from endogenous topology: new processes, customers, links, task coverage, embodiments, and firm boundaries. Do not claim to model reorganization if only edge weights change while all relevant connections remain fixed.

### 3.3 Customer demand is endogenous

Separate demand for the customer's own output from derived demand for robotic services. Include feedback from deployment into costs, capacity, product quality, lead times, output decisions, prices, growth, and subsequent robot demand. Allow adverse as well as reinforcing loops.

Distinguish economic willingness to pay, financial ability to pay, procurement/implementation authority, and the price the provider can capture. Derive value from the best feasible alternative, not only the human wage associated with a task.

A starting fee ceiling is the difference between customer profit with robotic service before its fee and customer profit under the best feasible alternative, over a matched horizon. Account consistently for integration, retained work, support, risk, quality, output, and downstream constraints. Additional output earns incremental contribution, not gross revenue counted as profit. Do not count the same time saving simultaneously as removed labor and increased output without a feasible allocation.

Represent final-demand limits and competition. A growing market is not automatically a high-margin customer; a technically productive deployment may not create saleable additional output. Separate market-share capture from growth in the overall market.

### 3.4 Capital allocation changes future possibilities

Treat the provider/operator as choosing among heterogeneous investments: existing fleet expansion; manufacturing and supplier capacity; reliability/maintenance; data collection/teleoperation/simulation/training; digital coordination/integration; new hardware/tooling/embodiments; customer development/process redesign; and retained liquidity or a staged experiment.

Differentiate lead times, irreversibility, complementarity, depreciation, risk, and financing eligibility. Model investment sequences, not merely isolated project returns.

Cash, data, capabilities, and installed physical capital have different transferability. Keep payer-specific cash constraints even when describing consolidated economics. Internal fees cancel in consolidated resource accounting but may still affect which legal entity can fund an obligation.

Use continuation value to represent future opportunities. Do not add an arbitrary 'strategic option premium' on top of a value function that already includes those future benefits. Distinguish learning about an unknown opportunity from physically creating capability that opens an opportunity. Stage, defer, abandon, redeploy, and expand should be genuine choices where relevant.

### 3.5 Learning, hardware, and customer access coevolve

Separate policy/perception/control learning, engineering/design knowledge, manufacturing learning, organizational learning, and commercial discovery. Operational data, teleoperation, simulation, public/pretrained knowledge, and targeted experiments can contribute differently and cost differently.

Transfer must be directional, configuration-specific, and potentially weak, asymmetric, negative, or unavailable. Distinguish skill similarity from empirically demonstrated transfer. Include distribution shift, diversity, diminishing returns, data processing, adaptation, validation, and access rights at the level needed for the result.

Treat embodiment choice as a real design/qualification decision with fixed costs, time, support requirements, and physical constraints. 'New embodiment' need not mean a wholly new humanoid: grippers, fixtures, sensors, mobility, payload, tooling, and simplified specialized machines can matter.

Separate cumulative manufacturing learning, current production scale, design-to-cost, procurement terms, and component substitution. Fleet growth is not itself evidence of each mechanism. A lower new-unit cost does not retroactively reduce historical cash paid for installed cohorts.

Keep generalist, specialized, human, digital-only, fixed-automation, and mixed-fleet alternatives. Do not predetermine that humanoids or vertical integration win.

### 3.6 RaaS and organizational boundaries

Relate revenue to actual billable activity and contract terms, not installed fleet alone. Compare hourly, output-based, reserved-capacity/minimum-commitment, and other contracts only where they change a mechanism. Faster robots can require fewer billable hours for a fixed output under an hourly contract; pricing and demand responses must therefore be explicit.

Separate competence improvements from coordination improvements. Ask when either changes feasible fleet size, service mix, supervision needs, and the value of another deployment. Include shared-resource congestion and correlated failures where material.

Compare integrated providers with contracted OEM/model/operator/financier structures and customer ownership. Shared intelligence need not require common legal ownership. Better coordination could favor integration or make specialization easier. Keep customer ownership/organization distinct from provider-side asset ownership/business models.

## 6. Stage 2 — Construct the smallest models that deliver original insight

**Schedule:** 16 November–6 December 2026. Deliver explicit Models A/B and an exact worked comparison or switching-condition attempt by 22 November; the first result or consequential rejected conjecture plus counterexample search by 29 November; and the first-paper contribution decision by 6 December. Keep models tractable enough to derive and understand; the full Model D is optional unless the central result needs it.

Develop a model ladder rather than one all-encompassing simulator. Add a state or mechanism only when it answers a stated question or changes a result.

**Model A: static service and surplus benchmark.** Define accepted output, a feasible incumbent alternative, customer demand/value, provider costs, pricing, and payer cash. Reuse established accounting. Establish the current-period deployment ranking and identify when it differs from a wage-based or hardware-cost ranking.

**Model B: two-period deployment and investment choice.** Begin with two contrasting applications, an existing embodiment, finite deployable cash, and an investment that affects a later opportunity. Endogenize the relevant demand or contract decision, with other strategic responses held fixed and disclosed. Compare a current-margin policy with a forward-looking policy. Derive a switching condition, comparative static, or exact counterexample analytically where possible.

**Model C: learning and embodiment expansion.** Add directed learning transfer, qualification/development costs, and a choice between scaling an existing configuration and creating/modifying another. Distinguish learning for productivity from learning that expands eligibility. Introduce uncertainty or beliefs only when needed to study experimentation, waiting, or option value; specify the information structure and avoid arbitrary empirical probabilities.

**Model D: coordination, organization, and competitive response.** Add shared supervision/digital coordination, contracts/rights, organization, or competitor response one at a time. Distinguish a single-provider decision problem from an equilibrium. Do not call a centrally optimized multi-firm model decentralized competition. The first paper need not solve full dynamic general equilibrium.

For each model, state time, units, agents, states, controls, demand, technology, information, budgets, transition laws, and objective. Specify the market structure and which prices are endogenous. Address terminal value, remaining assets, support obligations, and finite-horizon artifacts.

A possible state includes cash by actor, fleet by qualified configuration/cohort, capability/knowledge, manufacturing experience, customer/demand state, contracts/rights, and the opportunity network. This is a candidate state list, not permission to include all dimensions before they are needed.

Conjectures to investigate, not conclusions to manufacture:

- Current-profit rankings reverse when deployments differ in transferable learning and future market access.
- Finance and customer commitments change the optimal order of capability expansion even holding engineering potential fixed.
- Demand depth, pricing, and task composition redirect data accumulation and the future automation frontier.
- Scale can favor either a reusable generalist platform or a specialized successor embodiment, depending on transfer, volume, costs, and support.
- Coordination improvements change the marginal value of more hardware or broader task coverage.
- Ownership and data/payment contracts alter which party can capture and finance an otherwise valuable expansion.
- More deployment data or capability is not necessarily more private profit, more consumer surplus, or more total welfare; distinguish these objectives.

Do not treat 'invest in learning when future benefits exceed current costs' as the original contribution. That is a generic dynamic-investment observation. Seek a structure-specific result: for example, how misalignment between production, learning-transfer, and payment networks changes deployment rankings; how complementary capabilities create market-entry thresholds; or how demand and qualification costs determine generalist versus specialized embodiment investment. Explain what a reader learns that a generic exploration–exploitation or real-options model would not already supply. The exact two-period example is a starting diagnostic, not by itself a publishable novelty claim.

Attempt proofs for tractable versions; actively search for counterexamples and boundary cases. Distinguish necessary from sufficient conditions, local from global results, existence from uniqueness, and generic comparative statics from an illustrative parameterization. Use symbolic/numerical tools to check—not substitute for—arguments.

**Outputs:** model definitions; derivation notebook; proposition/conjecture ledger; a worked example with an exact decision comparison; counterexamples; and an explanation of what is genuinely new relative to the seed literature.

**Exit criterion:** at least one consequential, well-specified result or rejected conjecture that does not amount solely to an accounting identity. Do not require an arbitrary theorem count. A rigorous computational contribution can be valuable, but its domain must be explicit.

Here 'topological empirics' means measuring economically meaningful network structure and testing implications of that structure. Do not introduce topological data analysis, persistent homology, or graph embeddings unless they solve a defined measurement problem better than simpler methods.

Build three linked resolutions:

1. **Sector/value-chain backbone:** official input-output, employment/occupation, and related public data to locate production relationships and labor exposure. 2. **Workflow/configuration cases:** source-backed production sequences, accepted outputs, complementary work, incumbent alternatives, handoffs, adoption constraints, and customer/provider roles.
3. **Capability/learning adjacency:** map which configurations serve which work and which directed transfers are measured, plausible but untested, contradicted, or unknown. Technical similarity is not observed learning transfer.

Use a sparse, inspectable schema. For a node/edge record preserve identity, relation type/direction, time/configuration, unit, quantity/range when available, source locator, observed versus inferred status, and uncertainty. Missing is not zero. Keep hypothesized and counterfactual edges distinct from observed edges, including visually.

Select contrasting cases because they distinguish mechanisms, not because they are the most convenient existing dossiers. Candidate comparisons include related handling across manufacturing and fulfillment; logistics across healthcare and warehouses; or inspection that changes subsequent maintenance work. Retain existing hospital, utility, and automotive evidence where useful without letting it dictate the entire scope. Transfer between these settings must be tested, not assumed. Include both within-market expansion and cross-market reuse.

Construct an opportunity matrix with two dimensions: existing versus additional task capability, and existing versus new end market/value-chain position. For each candidate expansion record required capability/configuration; remaining engineering/qualification/commercial investment; customer economics and financing; alternatives; potential price/volume; provider cost/cash; rights; learning benefits; and uncertainty. Do not convert incomplete rows into a falsely precise universal score.

Compute only interpretable quantities: bottleneck exposure, complementary capability bundles, common scarce resources, overlap of reachable opportunity sets, economically weighted transfer paths, concentration of dependence, and candidate expansion sequences. Derive why a metric predicts an economic outcome before using it as a ranking. Unweighted centrality is not evidence of a valuable deployment.

Design empirical tests from the theory. Specify the estimand, unit of observation, treatment/exposure, outcome, confounders, selection mechanisms, and required variation. Separate descriptive mapping from causal inference. Learning-curve work must consider concurrent design changes, time trends, selection, and reverse causality. Cross-market transfer needs matched evaluation or appropriately bounded evidence.

Do not manufacture calibration or treat an illustrative graph as an economy-wide estimate. Select a held-out or negative case where possible so the narrative is not built exclusively from cases that fit.

Suggested structure:

1. Introduction: the economic puzzle, central question, result, and precise contribution.
2. A worked example: how a current-period deployment ranking can differ from the dynamic choice, and the conditions required.
3. Related literature: closest models and the exact changes made here, not a detached survey.
4. Economic environment: agents, technology, demand, information, contracts, learning, and investment constraints.
5. Core analysis: propositions/comparative statics, proofs or proof sketches, and intuition.
6. Dynamic or computational extension: the minimum added complexity needed for the main question.
7. Empirical topology and application: measured relationships, illustrative mapping, identifiable implications, and explicit limits.
8. Robustness and counterexamples: where conclusions reverse or fail.
9. Implications and next tests: bounded deployment/organization lessons and a falsifiable follow-on agenda.
10. Conclusion: what is learned and what remains unsettled.
