## The common question

How does an allocation of physical robots, digital agents and complementary resources change current production, the production of future capability, the return to additional capital, and the next allocation of production across firms and industries?

Keep separate: task performance; usable evidence; validated and transferable capability; accepted output; physical marginal products; private investment returns; and aggregate real surplus. Explain the relationships among them rather than turning them into one scalar “intelligence” stock.

The current common paper already derives a matrix of induced deployments for a local regime. The week's main advance should explain how its coefficients, admissible links and bottlenecks are themselves generated by firm choices, learning and market responses. Re-deriving its fixed-coefficient threshold is not sufficient.

## Five priorities

| Priority | Central question | Required theoretical development | Connection to the common model |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Production of capability | What turns production, failure, intervention, simulation or deliberate investigation into reusable capability, and when does success erode its own evidence supply? | Specify heterogeneous evidence and capabilities, endogenous sampling/admission/retention, directional transfer, diminishing novelty, forgetting, embodiment compatibility and qualification. Distinguish learning a task, learning which test to run, and changing the procedure that chooses tests. | Derive the learning map and its dependence on deployments, allocation and the existing corpus; do not assume a fixed positive transfer matrix everywhere. |
| 2. Multivariate MPK_r and capital velocity | How do capability gains change the physical and private returns to different robot investments, and when do they accelerate or enlarge deployment? | Define accepted-service marginal products by site, embodiment, market and date. Separate operating throughput, commissioning speed and recycling of working capital. Solve binding complements, impact versus adjusted-fleet responses, opportunity costs and finite investment differences. | Derive the installation response from production, demand, resource and cash constraints, not from an arbitrary data premium. |
| 3. Recursive allocation and organizational learning | When does a firm profit by changing how it chooses experiments, transfers, release rules and budgets? | Model a retained allocation/learning procedure as an endogenous object. Compare with a strong fixed organization that can respond to state and learn ordinary task skills, and with equivalent contingent contracting. Charge verification, delay and redesign. | Make the transition law and information structure objects of investment; identify when revisability adds value beyond a good predesigned rule. |
| 4. Propagation and heterogeneous diffusion | How do changes travel upstream, downstream and across markets through production, learning, capital and release authority? | Extend the common network beyond one positive, constant regime. Study saturation, correlated evidence and demand, shared resources, endogenous regime changes, signed responses, missing complements and feedback delays. Distinguish speed, eventual extent and temporary displacement. | Couple firm decisions into dated network responses and distinguish locally valid thresholds from global diffusion claims. |
| 5. Competition, rents and firm structure | Who captures gains, who invests, and how do rivals and input suppliers change the loops? | Endogenize a focused competitive or price response. Examine learning incentives, pass-through, rents to scarce complements, entry and specialization, data diffusion, partial versus full integration, and different degrees of autonomy. | Recompute private incentives and network links after responses; do not equate technical spillovers, private profits and total production. |

Applied comparisons should span at least three economically distinct environments where the mechanism warrants them: repetitive material handling; manufacturing with several complementary stages and integration costs; inspection/maintenance with sparse events and delayed outcomes; and digital exception handling as a useful comparator. Arms, mobile robots, humanoids, quadrupeds and drones are an option set, not a requirement to manufacture identical evidence for every embodiment.

## Method and intellectual standard

For each central result, write the sequence:

1. Economic puzzle and a concrete production setting.
2. Technological/institutional premises, payoff units, agents, information and constraints.
3. State, controls, timing and the feasible ordinary alternative, which also learns.
4. Mechanism and derivation; distinguish definitions, mathematical identities, propositions and conjectures.
5. Comparative statics, boundary conditions and an actual counterexample or rival account.
6. Propagation to another input, market, claimant, allocation or time horizon.
7. Observable implications and an empirical contrast that could reject the account.
8. A small reproducible model or numerical example only where it resolves a question.

Use Data-Driven Automation and Organizing Intelligence as methodological comparators and precise prior art, not as mandatory functional forms. Verify primary sources when using their claims. Preserve the full broader RoboEcon agenda and avoid overfitting to either paper. Ordinary learning, capable incumbents and optional retention of existing policies belong in the comparator.

A priori reasoning is welcome. It must expose premises and derive consequences. Missing industrial data does not prevent theory; it prevents claiming measured magnitudes. A formal result can be valuable without a novelty claim. Neither a source list nor a simulation count establishes a theoretical contribution.
