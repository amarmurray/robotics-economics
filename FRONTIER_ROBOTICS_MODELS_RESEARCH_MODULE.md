# Frontier robotics models: the economics of embodied intelligence

Version 1.0 · Accepted scope 25 October 2026 · Workstream K · Added to an active campaign

## Purpose and industrial position

Investigate the labs, model developers, OEM research teams, deployment organizations, open policy ecosystems, integrators and capital providers that produce and distribute increasingly general-purpose robotic intelligence. The key object is not a company roster or an abstract model benchmark but **the development, adaptation, use and monetization of intelligence as a productive input to physical services**.

Central question: **Who develops, owns, distributes, adapts, improves and captures the economic gains from robotic intelligence, and when can a model supplier exist independently of robot manufacturing and customer operations?**

Workstream K is a substantive addition alongside A–J, linking directly to capability/scaling (B), learning/data rights (D), fleet/site coordination (E), customer governance/AI-native newcos (F), providers and asset/value capture (G), LLM and digital models (H), physical supply/embodiment (I), and quadruped/drone evidence (J). The primary economic cell remains end market × value-chain stage × operating archetype × geography × time. Use shared actor, model/system, customer, deployment, source and scenario IDs: a lab may also be a manufacturer or operator without creating duplicate capacity or revenue.

**Running-campaign integration constraint.** The user's research campaign is already active and the user is conserving usage by running a single Astra Max agent. This addition neither restarts its recorded T0/deadline nor instructs the running agent to spawn collaborators, stop its existing validated work, or train models. ## 1. Map the differentiated commercial and technical layers

| Structure | What must be mapped | Economic hypothesis to test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Independent model laboratory | Research, policy, training and adaptation, licensing, API/hosted inference, OEM/customer compatibility and contracts | A specialist intelligence provider could serve many embodied devices without owning the entire hardware/site. |
| Model-led deployer/integrator | Model company also supplies commissioning, customer workflow, managed services or acquired deployment assets | Customer access and field data may improve models but add service capital, staffing and delivery risk. |
| Integrated OEM | Robot-maker develops models, owns sensor/actuator interfaces and may collect deployment data | Co-design and control may justify duplicate model-R&D cost; benefits and exclusivity vary by workload. |
| Compute/platform company | Models bundled with AI training, simulation, data tooling, cloud or onboard chips | Open complements may increase platform demand; model and chip rents must be separated. |
| Open policy ecosystem | Code, weights, licenses, datasets, model recipes, harness, middleware and hardware interfaces | Availability may enable new suppliers/operators; practical adaptation, hardware qualification and support remain necessary. |
| Customer/internal autonomy team | Customer retains its own workflows, specialist policies, local adaptation, deployment operation and data | Field-specific learning can offset the benefits of a centralized model, depending on transfer and pricing. |

Map relationships separately: equity funding, cloud/compute purchases, model licenses, SDK access, customer acquisition, reseller channels, robot deployments, data reuse rights, training partnerships, service obligations, talent movement and actual acquisitions. Distinguish demonstrations, commercial agreements, paid operating customers, installed devices, accepted productive output, revenue, margin and realized investment returns.

## 3. Model development, training inputs and physical scaling

Research the complete intelligence-production chain: human video, real-robot demonstrations, teleoperation, simulated rollouts, tactile/force/proprioceptive data, annotations, labeling and evals, policy/model architectures, pretraining, post-training, RL, human feedback, in-context physical prompting, inference effort and on-robot adaptation.

Separate **training compute** from **inference/serving compute** and **onboard control hardware**. Record GPU/memory/power/network availability, data rights, costs and critical-path R&D time. Changes to model structure can alter feasible chips, latency, battery heat and component needs in Workstream I. A common datacenter resource is not identical to a compatible onboard memory part or actuator.

Study response surfaces conditional on task, environment, embodiment, distribution, model+controller/harness, data, training budget, evidence duration and human intervention. Examine held-out cross-body/task transfer, negative transfer, reliability and recovery, time to integrate, accepted work and generalization. Avoid universal scaling exponents or translating a short-horizon success rate into firm productivity. A model release, benchmark demonstration and supported production fleet are separate objects.

## 4. Product, economics, financing and value capture

Compare licensing per robot/site, policy subscriptions, managed fine-tuning, cloud inference, local inference, usage/outcome pricing, OEM embedding, model-led deployment/operation, RaaS, acquisition/JV and open model adaptation. Trace who owns the model, fine-tune, application data, deployment feedback, monitoring, control layer, asset, customer and service relationship.

For matched accepted output/quality, compare full customer/operator/provider economics: body and payload, edge/remote inference, data collection, training amortization, integration and process redesign, safety/validation, latency/network fallback, commissioning, maintenance, human review, supervision, retries, downtime, model payments, support, replacement, financing and residual value. Keep customer savings distinct from laboratory revenue, integrator contribution and net surplus; eliminate internal charges in consolidated views.

Ask whether greater generality lowers deployment-specific engineering labor or creates ongoing support obligations; whether improvements diffuse to competitors through a shared model; when data access and field expertise create durable advantage; and whether proprietary model performance translates into bargaining power given substitute models and OEM/customer negotiating options.

Map capital access, lab R&D spending, chip/inference commitments, field data-collection investment, robot access, acquisitions and distribution separately. Report vendor funding/ARR/customer counts with dates, scope and uncertainty, not as independently audited operating margin. Explore competition, integration, model openness/commoditization, switching costs and changing value-chain boundaries.

## 5. Learning networks, rights and technical boundaries

A deployer's number of installed robots is not equal to effective training-data diversity or validated learning improvement. Trace episode capture → access and allowed reuse → filtering and correction → training/evaluation → model release → compatibility and upgrade → useful operating effect → beneficiary. Track latency in that loop, cost, negative transfer, local specialization and data privacy/security. Ask whether repeated specialized deployments contribute marginally to a general base model and how one lab/OEM/customer captures or shares that gain.

Separate learned motion policies, high-level vision-language reasoning, conventional optimization, fleet control, enterprise agents, ERP/WMS/MES integration and low-level deterministic control. Document who receives state, proposes or executes actions, signs off transactions, handles failure and maintains physical deadlines. An announced cross-embodiment foundation model is not evidence of an end-to-end autonomous organization.

Use Workstream H's multivariate analogy ledger for comparisons to digital model labs, APIs, open weights, deploycos and capital/input competition. Test necessary similarities and material physical differences; classify analogies, demonstrated integration and common resource inputs separately.

## 6. Four-architecture deployment counterfactual

At least one informative end-market/value-chain cell should compare four system architectures, using one consistent service outcome and quality requirement:

1. OEM-integrated proprietary policy and robot.
2. Same or suitably matched robot using a licensed independent frontier model with explicit adaptation/inference terms.
3. Open policy plus necessary hardware, engineering, fine-tuning, deployment and support.
4. Model-led deployment provider or operating entity that assumes more workflow/field responsibility.

Retain conventional/digitally improved human and specialized automation baselines. State infeasible cells rather than forcing a match. Sensitize data availability, fine-tuning cycles, intervention, uptime, latency/edge compute, price, service and rights. Separately compare a customer-side AI-native newco and an incumbent with equal access to technology; test adoption-timing versus process-design versus durable learning/distribution advantages.

Connect resulting changes to model supplier margins, OEM share, customer adoption, constrained manufacturing/service capacity, physical-labor effects and potential new demand; avoid double-counting the same work, learning or transfer payment.
