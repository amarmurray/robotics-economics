# Digital AI ecosystem: comparative evidence and direct contributions to robotics

Version 1.0 · Prepared 25 October 2026 · Research specification; campaign not activated

## 1. Purpose, scope, and relationship to the program

Workstream H is a substantial investigation of the digital AI ecosystem and its relationship to physical production. Study language and multimodal models, agents, their development and supply chains, deployment and nondeployment, productive consequences, competition, and dependent businesses. Develop this understanding as an economic research object while using it to improve decisions in Robotics Economics.

The module has two connected purposes:

1. **Disciplined comparison:** test which digital-AI mechanisms illuminate humanoid capability, deployment, industry structure, financing, and value capture.
2. **Direct contribution:** investigate how digital models and agents change robotic control, site and enterprise operations, coordination, and the development of humanoid systems.

A finding can serve both purposes, but each requires its own evidentiary argument. Digital adoption is not a calibrated forecast of physical adoption. Technical integration is not proof that two industries have matching economics.

H is the eighth workstream, alongside A–G. End markets, value-chain stages, operating archetypes, geography, and time remain the organizing dimensions. Benchmarks and individual tasks provide diagnostic evidence. RESEARCH_PROGRAM_DESIGN.md governs the whole program; WEEK_ONE_RESEARCH_SPEC.md governs the initial campaign; RESEARCH_ROADMAP.md retains stable open questions.

This document specifies research and introduces dated source seeds. It claims no completed ecosystem census, calibrated forecast, research run, model result, or deployed dashboard. Public-source investigation, CPU analysis, and local software are within scope. Training, GPU expenditure, hardware experiments, paid access, outreach, and changes to the separately scoped implementation repository are not activated by this module.

## 3. Capability and operating economics

### Model development and inference

Separate research expenditure, training, post-training, evaluation, inference, integration, and ongoing operation. Investigate how model choice, routing, inference effort, tools, caching, verification, and workflow design change quality, latency, and cost. Use the relevant configured system, not a model name alone.

Fit scaling relationships only within evidenced domains. Identify the resource budget, data distribution, training method, evaluation, and operating conditions. Model quality, agent performance, and enterprise output are different response variables. Do not import an exponent across these levels or assume that a laboratory scaling result determines market adoption.

Measure operating cost per accepted output where possible: model/service usage, tooling, orchestration, human review, interventions, retries, error correction, integration amortization, infrastructure, and delay. Report acceptance criteria and failure severity. Token volume and benchmark scores are inputs or indicators, not productive output.

Investigate both supplier and customer economics. Lower usage prices may affect demand, margins, and resource requirements differently. Increased utilization or demand expansion must be modeled rather than inferred from declining unit cost. Competition may change pricing, service bundles, and which participant captures an improvement.

### Deployment and nondeployment

Distinguish demonstrated capability, reproducibility in a target workflow, economic attractiveness, organizational readiness, actual adoption, and realized outcomes. Define any capability overhang against a specified workflow population, service standard, date, and evidence threshold.

Investigate nondeployment through competing explanations: capability uncertainty, reliability, error exposure, integration, unavailable complements, authority, capital allocation, managerial incentives, customer demand, and implementation capacity. Do not automatically assign unexplained nonadoption to resistance or an ownership category.

Separate task-level experimental effects, workflow changes, firm outcomes, and end-market consequences. Retrospective comparisons must consider selection, simultaneous process redesign, changing model versions, demand, and treatment intensity. Prospective scenarios must expose these dependencies rather than mechanically extending measured task effects to employment or industry growth.

### Shared resources and feedback

Allocate training and inference demands against compatible chip, memory, networking, data-center, power, and financing resources. Resource pools are not universally interchangeable. Distinguish capital commitments from cash, theoretical capacity from usable capacity, and land access from an operational site.

Trace digital AI's possible effects on robotics research and deployment, then robotics' possible effects on construction, maintenance, manufacturing, logistics, and infrastructure supporting digital AI. Include complementary resources and lead times in each direction. Treat reinforcing feedback as a hypothesis, not an assumption of unconstrained acceleration.

## 4. Analogy-test contract

Maintain a dedicated analogy ledger. Each proposed transfer requires:

1. **Source and target:** identify actors, market/stage, time, outcome, and economic boundary on each side.
2. **Mechanism:** state the causal path and intermediate variables; avoid resemblance based only on labels.
3. **Required similarities:** identify necessary conditions, including incentives, resource availability, information, coordination, and competition.
4. **Material differences:** examine replication costs, capital intensity, physical deployment, local adaptation, reliability, intervention, asset life, data collection, and switching.
5. **Source evidence:** state what supports the digital mechanism, including selection, measurement, and causal limitations.
6. **Target evidence:** assess the robotics conditions independently; absence of evidence is not confirmation.
7. **Prediction and falsifier:** specify an observable implication and findings that would weaken or reject it.
8. **Permitted use:** identify whether the analogy informs a question, qualitative prior, model structure, or numerical parameter.
9. **Alternatives and revision:** retain competing explanations, contradictory observations, and subsequent changes.

Use explicit statuses such as supported source mechanism, plausible untested target transfer, missing target conditions, contradicted, and insufficient evidence. A single score should not conceal these distinctions. Numerical transfer requires target justification beyond the similarity of industry narratives.

Initial candidate tests should include at least six mechanisms, such as scaling and delivered productivity; model commoditization and integration value; deployment access and learning; inexpensive replication versus physical rollout; benchmark gains and operating reliability; and lab-linked platforms versus robotic operators. Include material boundaries and plausible failed transfers. A rejected analogy is a useful result.

For example, an evidenced decline in the price of a digital capability can motivate investigation of robot reasoning costs. It cannot establish the decline rate of total physical production cost, which also depends on hardware, maintenance, throughput, supervision, and site conditions.

## 5. Direct contributions and overlapping deployment stages

Produce three separate contribution maps. Each map identifies input capability, interface, affected decision or process, operating constraints, measurable effect, evidence, and remaining hypothesis.

### A. Robot reasoning and control

Investigate perception, semantic interpretation, task decomposition, action selection, tool use, recovery, and coordination with lower-level policies. Identify which component proposes an action, which executes it, what feedback is observed, and where timing, state estimation, physical dynamics, and reliability constrain the connection.

Distinguish action-policy research from actual robot execution. Simulated success under paused inference or unrestricted retries cannot establish wall-clock productivity. High-level orchestration and low-level motor control require different measurements. Track model, policy, embodiment, environment, control frequency, intervention, and evaluation conditions.

### B. Fleet, site, and enterprise operations

Investigate task allocation, routing, scheduling, inventory, procurement, staffing, quality, maintenance, and exception handling across robots, people, and digital systems. Include interfaces to enterprise resource planning, warehouse management, manufacturing execution, fleet managers, and site infrastructure.

For each interface, define the state read, command or plan produced, actor with authority, acknowledgement, failure signal, recovery process, and record updated. Distinguish conventional integration, model-assisted decisions, and autonomous execution. Having an interface does not establish that an LLM improves it; an announced agent feature does not establish operational deployment or productivity.

Examine local versus enterprise objectives, stale or inconsistent state, congestion, supervisory queues, correlated errors, coordination overhead, and accountability. Compare orchestration mechanisms against simpler policies using equivalent information and resources. More agents or longer reasoning is not assumed to improve performance.

### C. Humanoid research and development

Investigate contributions to coding, simulation construction, experiment design, reward design, domain randomization, data annotation, evaluation, debugging, component selection, and knowledge synthesis. Trace proposed research acceleration to validated changes in capability, development cost, or cycle time.

Separate generated candidates from verified improvements, simulator results from physical transfer, and results in bounded tasks from general humanoid development. Include human review, failed proposals, validation expense, compute, and experimental bottlenecks. This campaign reviews evidence and models mechanisms; it does not initiate training or physical experiments.

### Staging and the changing counterfactual

Digital preparation, physical deployment, fleet coordination, and enterprise redesign can overlap. Do not impose a universal digital-then-robot ladder. Digital systems may precede robots, improve concurrently, or become useful only with sufficient physical capacity.

Use a 2×2 comparison with matched output and service requirements:

| | No added digital-AI intervention | Specified digital-AI intervention |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Human-led physical operations | Current baseline, including existing conventional software | Improved human-led operations |
| Specified robot-assisted operations | Incremental robotics with required conventional integration | Combined physical–digital system |

Let Yrd denote an outcome with robotics state r and additional digital-intervention state d. Compare robotics under digital improvement as Y11−Y01 and the interaction as Y11−Y10−Y01+Y00. Specify whether higher values mean improvement; retain separate output and cost measures. These are scenario contrasts until evidence supports causal interpretation.

Hold required embedded robot software fixed across the robot row when evaluating an additional digital intervention. If the intervention changes the robot capability itself, state that system-boundary change. Missing or infeasible cells remain explicit rather than inventing an independently operable robot baseline.

Evaluate robot adoption against the evolving digital-improved incumbent, as well as the historical baseline. Count joint benefits once. Expand demand, change output mix, or alter organizational scope only in separately identified scenarios; otherwise the comparison cannot isolate the proposed contributions.

## 6. Competition, dependent firms, and integration with A–G

Record lab relationships across API/model access, equity investment, governance, contracts, distribution, cloud purchases, infrastructure financing, technical support, data rights, and customer access. These are distinct connections, not a single tethering score. Track switching costs, portability, alternative providers, concentration, bargaining, and changes over time.

Investigate how improvements in models and interoperability change the need for complementary firms: some functions may be absorbed into a model platform, some may move between layers, and others may become more valuable. Test whether deployment access produces informative experience, evaluation opportunities, or product improvements under the applicable rights and feedback arrangements. Customer count alone does not establish a learning advantage. Compare standards and modular interfaces with integrated stacks as technical and commercial arrangements whose effects require evidence.

Examine deployment companies by actual role: implementation services, integration, operation, investment, business ownership, or a combination. Distinguish formation, announced acquisitions, completed transactions, operating deployments, and financial outcomes. A commercial announcement supports the disclosed arrangement, not its effectiveness.

Use one actor/role and rights map across H, F, and G. A lab-affiliated company may be a customer, intermediary, operator, and investor. Preserve company and deployment identities, avoid duplicating capital or demand across roles, and eliminate internal transfers from consolidated economics. Do not assume that ownership creates access to every dataset or operating decision.

## 7. Evidence and record requirements

Match evidence to claims. Capability claims require versioned experiments and evaluation conditions; economic effects require appropriate operating or causal evidence; resource claims require capacity definitions and dates; relationships require primary disclosures. Preserve vendor-reported results, independent observations, research findings, interpretations, and scenarios as separate classes.

Benchmark cards must record system/harness versions, task population, budget, tool access, human involvement, retries, acceptance criteria, error severity, timing, costs, and external-validity limits. Investigate contamination, selection, and measurement changes. A benchmark can inform a component without validating an occupation or enterprise.

Reuse existing market, chain, actor, system, evidence, scenario, and question IDs. Add linked records for digital workloads, evaluations, resource capacity, deployment episodes, business dependencies, analogy tests, and direct-contribution mechanisms. Each record needs dates, applicability, uncertainty, and source locators. Keep source-side and target-side evidence fields separate.

Record treatment and comparator for impact claims. Record announcements separately from availability and actual use. Preserve null, conflicting, and negative findings. Unknown quantities remain null with reasons; illustrative assumptions must not populate empirical totals.

Explain implications for at least **three existing decisions or research questions**: for example market sequencing, deployment architecture, learning priorities, customer-entry feasibility, or provider boundaries.
