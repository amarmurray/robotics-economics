# A staffing saving needs a feasible complete workload

Editions 0.35–0.37 · synthetic execution and decision challenge, not empirical deployment economics.

The [hospital economic audit](field_support_denominators.md#hospital-economic-claims-edition-034) made the missing organizational bridge explicit: released minutes do not identify paid staffing that can disappear. The new [shared-workforce contract](../model/SHARED_WORKFORCE.md) executes that bridge conditionally. A single paid site crew must cover local robot attention, inventory jobs, robot handling and manual cleaning. A notice policy can reduce a future commitment; actual funds buy extra capacity; both service obligations and every payer remain visible.

The first decision is whether the whole workload is feasible. The second is whether its total resource and payment consequences support the deployment. A favorable answer on one staffing line cannot answer both questions.

## What changes the choice

The fixed-crew and notice cases both deliver all 4,000 cleaning zones and 160 separate inventory jobs over eight months. Under the declared August notice taking effect in October, regular staffing cash falls from $32,000 to $22,000. Four months of notice instead retain $26,000. This is an executed synthetic commitment change, not a dollar value assigned to released minutes, a measured employment result or proof that a real contract permits the reduction. Transition costs beyond retained notice-period staffing are unknown.

The benefit is still conditional on other work. Doubling inventory demand in the last four months requires 1,240 extra paid crew minutes per affected month. Funding them costs $4,960 over the window and preserves complete service. With no extra capacity, the same regular staffing outlay buys only 2,000 cleaning zones and 232 of 240 required inventory jobs; only four months meet the complete obligation. Its smaller material/cash bill cannot support a savings claim. At an extra-capacity ceiling of 1,239 minutes, all inventory jobs are completed but cleaning falls to 3,998 zones. One missing minute per affected month is enough to fail the complete-service test.

The human comparator changes the strategic interpretation. The original digital-human path needs $7,680 of extra capacity to cover the same 4,000 zones and 160 jobs. A stronger assumed dispatch path reduces cleaning work sufficiently to fit the existing paid crew, leaving inventory work unchanged. It pays $32,000 of regular staffing and no extra capacity. Its bounded external resource cash is $43,900, compared with $223,289.36 for the robot notice case. The $10,000 staffing reduction therefore does not make the robot case the lower-cash alternative under these premises. Opening assets, closing liabilities/receivables, transition costs and terminal value remain outside any full lifecycle comparison; no real technology ranking or ROI follows.

## Shared work and shared money must both be real

Inventory work is no longer merely an unavailable-capacity reservation. It has required job IDs, actual completed/failed work, a rate, an independently funded buyer and dated invoices. With the inventory buyer unfunded, all 160 jobs can still be delivered from the operator's existing capital, while no receipts arrive: $9,600 remains uncollected, of which $8,400 is already due at the window end. The funded-buyer case still has a $1,200 future-due receivable. Neither a completed job nor an invoice becomes cash automatically.

Local robot attention and handling compete with manual cleaning and inventory work for the same identified crew. Professional model engineering, demonstrations, support and integration retain their distinct resources and invoices. Combining their labels into a notional shared labor pool would fabricate substitutability. Conversely, actual legal consolidation matters for funding: customer and operator logical capital share an account in the licensed profile. A zero allocation to one logical role does not exhaust the other role's cash. The adverse funding tests respect that identity.

The notice rule uses prior completed work and supported units, then waits through its declared delay. It cannot anticipate the later inventory surge or retroactively remove staff from earlier months. Once issued, a later shortfall does not cancel the modeled notice; the service ledger exposes the resulting extra-capacity need or missed work. A real deployment requires its own employment/contract authority, transition cost, working schedule and recovery options before this premise can support a decision.

The thin model-role funding world also passes the early notice condition but fails the last month's complete obligation. Later support leaves fewer operating robots, and the operator cannot prepay the extra crew minutes. A valid historical trigger therefore does not guarantee sustained staffing feasibility: maintenance funding, fallback liquidity and the staffing commitment must be assessed together over the intended horizon.

## Consequences for the three decision packs

For hospital logistics, qualify the entire request cohort and all retained handling, observation, dispatch, repair and fallback work before financing deployment from staffing savings. For utility inspection, include the residual human route and other paid crew duties. For automotive workcells, include feed, inspection, recovery and the crew's competing production obligations. These are measurement and accounting requirements; the cleaning/inventory rates, notice terms, prices and currencies do not transfer into those cells.

Proceed with a bounded comparison only when the required service, actual shared resource identity and funding can be specified. Wait when the business case relies on an unverified staffing transition or unpriced fallback. Reject a lower-cost comparison that misses required work, spends the same worker time twice, counts future receipts as available cash, or treats a proposed staffing cut as a measured payroll result. Keep the improved human/specialist alternative in the same decision set.

The initial edition 0.35 of [the complete export](../model/results/shared_workforce.json) contained nine replayable worlds with notice events, minute bookings, distinct service totals, role accounts and all invoices. It deepens DE-CF01 rather than inventing a new challenge family: fourteen families then contained 196 bounded worlds. These are independent comparisons, not one simultaneously feasible six-market portfolio. Full empirical cost, rights, organization and broad cross-module integration remain partial.

## Prospective purchase reserve, edition 0.37

The [historical workforce audit](field_support_denominators.md#employment-operating-windows-and-fallback-edition-036) identified the need for jointly staffed receiving duties and fallback. This extension tests one funding response inside the same execution. It does not turn a source's manual contingency into available workers. Nine additional worlds bring this package to eighteen; all nine original worlds retain their numerical outcomes. The same fourteen campaign challenge families now contain 205 bounded worlds, not one simultaneously feasible portfolio.

**The decision is a prospective operator cash floor on optional hardware purchase.** The independent OEM funds manufacture; the operator pays for delivered hardware and site crew time. At each shipment, the policy compares actual operator cash remaining after that purchase group with a fixed, declared floor. The activation month is also fixed at opening. Neither future workload nor future receipts enter this decision. The policy keeps existing support, commissioning, mandatory liabilities and overtime ordering intact. These payments can consume retained cash: the floor is a purchase rule, not escrow, an expense, a worker guarantee or an optimized liquidity requirement. Refusal counts describe repeated decisions and can include already-unaffordable quotes; inspect the matched zero-floor case to identify the policy effect.

The constrained-site comparison starts with $80,000 in the legally combined customer/operator account, the existing two robots and a later rise from twenty to forty inventory jobs per month. With no floor, one new robot is purchased in November and only commissioned in February. That last month's extra crew booking cannot be prepaid: only seven months satisfy both services, with 3,530 of 4,000 cleaning zones and 238 of 240 inventory jobs delivered. A declared $20,000 purchase floor refuses the deliveries, retains the existing two-robot fleet and funds $4,480 of extra crew time. It delivers all 4,000 zones and 240 jobs. Regular staffing remains $22,000 in both cases; there is no retroactive notice reversal or free replacement staff.

The operator's protection has a counterparty consequence. Both worlds already manufactured two units for $72,000. Under the reserve they remain finished OEM stock; the manufacture is not refunded or removed from resource cost. OEM closing cash is $128,000 rather than $187,900 after one sale and its shipment cost. Operator closing cash is $79,792.80 rather than $1,154.85. Purchase fees, OEM resource payments and retained assets are separate; no reserve amount is added to the consolidated bill.

A second case tests the previously thin model-led operator. A $40,000 floor active before the August purchase retains two supported robots and complete service. Starting the same policy in September cannot reverse the earlier acquisition or the binding staffing notice: the final service failure remains. The first purchase leaves exactly $38,418.16 available. A floor at that value admits it; one cent more refuses it and changes the later outcome. This is an exact test of the declared admission rule, not an empirically sufficient reserve or a search for the optimal policy. Existing obligations can still consume liquidity after admission.

Cash and capacity must both exist. Removing extra crew availability from the $20,000-reserve surge case leaves only four complete months, 2,000 zones and 236 inventory jobs, despite more than $55,000 remaining in the operator account. Conversely, an independent model supplier with no initial funding remains unable to qualify/support robots even while the operator holds cash. The paid human fallback covers that case under the declared overtime contract; no money is silently transferred between legal actors. These worlds assume qualified extra crew can respond within the current month up to its ceiling, with full prepayment. They establish neither actual availability nor a hiring, training or response-time entitlement.

A very high floor can reject useful expansion. With only one opening robot, admitting the two new units produces twenty supported robot-months and permits a later staffing notice, retaining $26,000 of regular staffing. A deliberately excessive $1 million floor leaves eight supported robot-months, more human cleaning and $32,000 of staffing. Both deliver complete service; the blocked-investment case still has lower bounded resource cash. The lost staffing option therefore does not establish that the investment would have earned a return.

The comparator remains consequential. The stronger assumed human-dispatch path covers the same 4,000-zone/240-job surge, paying $32,000 regular staffing and $9,600 extra capacity, with $53,500 total external resource cash. The reserve-protected robot case pays less crew cash but $165,773.92 total external resource cash. Opening hardware, terminal stock, future claims and unmodeled transition costs prevent a lifecycle or ROI ranking. The assumed human improvement also requires empirical qualification; it is not observed AI performance.

For the three decision packs, require a payer-specific budget and actual qualified response capacity, preserve the service receiving reassigned labor, and test late action and counterparty costs. Do not fund a hospital, utility or workcell decision with these cleaning rates or reserve values. The next campaign priority is the competition/entry/recovery interface and stronger digital-human alternatives across the six cells, not another series of hospital-specific reserve variants.

## Paid crews and actual customer awards, edition 0.41

The [joint crew execution](../model/results/staffed_renewals.json) now connects the staffing decision to actual recurring customer awards, company entry and separate operating accounts. It is one physical/Treasury execution. The existing architecture/resource workforce fixture and the asset-recovery fixture remain separate; their results are not added. Thirteen new worlds deepen the existing fourteen challenge families to **241 bounded worlds**. No empirical coefficient or staffing ratio enters this extension.

### The offer must fit the same crew that does the work

Each operating company has a unique qualified crew and legal payer. Regular staffing is a dated paid commitment. Before awards, each crew reserves local robot attention and its separately due inventory jobs. Remaining minutes support robot handling and human cleaning. The current robot cohort, ramp and digital-human work rate remain those of the existing calendar model. Raw robot output and the original human-capacity scalar cannot both consume the same minutes.

The order matters. A staffing notice uses only the prior completed month and supported fleet. Its reduction takes effect after the declared delay and remains binding through later shortfalls. A fixed optional overtime block is quoted in phase 1; mandatory bills and physical work occur before the full block is optionally paid in phase 5. The block targets the full fixed cleaning market before customer awards. Awards then consume feasible capacity and actual onboarding/switching funds; dispatch and new service invoices follow. Neither later receipts nor an expected award funds an earlier offer. Losing bids can leave prepaid capacity unused. This conventional policy is deliberately explicit and is not an optimized bid, staffing roster or legal employment rule.

Cleaning contracts are 50-zone lots in a fixed 500-zone monthly market. Inventory jobs have their own IDs, acceptance, deadline, payer and receipt lag; their quantities are never added to cleaning zones. Outside duties persist when cleaning contracts leave. Common ownership changes ultimate equity while operating cash, crew identities and liabilities remain separate. Another subsidiary's idle crew is not an authorized or qualified loan of staff.

### Results that change the decision

The full control serves **4,000 cleaning zones and 160 inventory jobs** over eight months. A notice issued in month 3 becomes effective in month 5. Cutting the target from two blocks to one lowers its feasible offer below a complete 500-zone market; 50 zones move to the rival. Both services still finish, with **$6,000 less regular staffing cash** and changed customer assignment. This is a bounded synthetic staffing transition, not measured payroll removal.

When inventory demand rises from 20 to 45 jobs after notice, the smaller crew performs only **242 of 260 jobs**, while cleaning falls to **3,400 zones**. Four-month locked contracts worsen cleaning to **2,590 zones** because work remains assigned to the constrained firm. A rival with a larger paid human crew restores all **4,000 cleaning zones**, but still leaves the same **18 inventory jobs undone**. A headline showing only cleaning completion would miss the failure of the retained service.

Prepaying a 5,000-minute overtime block for the last three months restores all 260 jobs and 4,000 zones. It costs **$15,000** of overtime against the $6,000 regular-staff reduction. Total resource/transaction cash from every payer is **$141,647.74**. A stronger human-only comparator with three regular target blocks serves the identical surge and cleaning demand for **$103,300**, with no robot manufacture or lease. The **$38,347.74** window difference is conditional on these synthetic rates, unused capacity, funding and ending assets/claims; it is not a lifecycle return. In the original 160-job workload, the competent human-only comparison also finishes everything at **$87,300**, versus **$127,197.74** for the robot with the staffing cut.

Two exact boundaries expose discontinuities. Human-only target capacity is exactly sufficient at two 4,800-minute blocks; reducing each block by one minute moves a whole 50-zone lot to the rival while market service remains complete. For a one-block target, **$7,850 opening cash** leaves exactly **$4,800** after initial mandatory bills for a 4,800-minute overtime block. One cent less prevents prepayment, leaves **50 zones unserved** in month 0 and creates later switching/onboarding costs. Across the window, the short-funded case spends **$112,500** in resources versus **$109,700** at the exact threshold, despite less service. Future receipts do not repair the missed period.

A lower rival offer can also win work after the incumbent prepays capacity. The price difference must exceed the customer's switching resources; an exact tie preserves the incumbent. A self-review caught an initial test premise that only tied the old contract. The adverse offer now crosses that threshold and verifies paid unused capacity after lost awards.

### Cash, scope and observation required

Staffing, overtime and outside receipts are already recorded in the enterprise ledger. The separate workforce view is a reconciliation, not another cost to add. All-payer resource/transaction cash includes outside customer switching bills and independently equals receipts of the modeled resource vendors. Company consideration, internal equipment payments, equity and loan principal are not added as resource consumption. Ending receivables, payables and physical assets remain visible; there is no residual valuation.

This advances the [labor-outcome gate](digital_labor_outcomes.md) without converting a survey answer into a staffing coefficient. The missing target observation is a dated roster, qualifications, retained duties, actual customer awards, notice/overtime obligations and every payer's cash through the same transition. A whole cleaning market, a viable operating company and complete retained work are distinct acceptance tests. The hospital, utility and automotive packs retain their own service units and rights; this cleaning contract supplies a mechanism challenge rather than a transfer of minutes or prices. Monthly divisible cleaning omits within-shift travel, sequencing and timed acceptance. Full six-cell portfolio feasibility, annual resource/parts composition, recovery, endogenous learning and empirical whole-system economics remain incomplete.

## Qualified support, parts and binding staffing, edition 0.44

[The qualified-staffing execution](../model/results/qualified_staffing.json) closes a specific interface left open by edition 0.41: annual provider support/integration and dated compatible parts now gate the same physical units that inform staffing notice, paid site work, company entry and actual customer awards. One physical loop and one Treasury execute all of them. This is a synthetic mechanism test, not a target staffing recommendation. The four architecture alternatives, asset-recovery and learning fixtures remain separate. Nine executions deepen existing challenge families to 250 bounded worlds; they are not nine additional empirical observations or a jointly feasible six-cell portfolio.

### Forward coverage matters after a successful month

The unreserved control preserves the previous binding-staff-cut history: 4,000 accepted cleaning zones, 160 inventory jobs, the same cash, title and customer-award history. Its one robot needs 650 minutes of provider support each operating month in 2026. Four supported months need exactly 2,600 available minutes. These provider engineers are different resources from the two operating companies' qualified site crews. Integration uses another pool. An annual reservation elsewhere makes capacity unavailable without inventing another executed job, employee or expense.

A successful September (month 2) permits notice in October (month 3), reducing the target's paid crew from two blocks to one in December (month 5). **2,600 versus 2,599 available provider minutes** changes December support: 649 minutes left cannot admit a whole 650-minute booking. The staff cut remains binding. The target can cover only three 50-zone lots after its inventory duties; the rival can cover six. One lot is unassigned and **50 zones go unserved**, while all 160 inventory jobs finish. January capacity restores robot service but cannot restore December's missed output. Thus eight complete months become seven even though the earlier notice test was satisfied.

Keeping the original paid staffing through the same support shortage restores all 4,000 zones and 160 jobs for **$131,806.45** of all-payer resource/transaction cash. The cut-and-shortfall case spends **$132,856.45** for less service. Its $6,000 lower regular staffing is outweighed by $7,100 of onboarding/customer-switching resource payments, partly offset by $50 less service material use. The **$1,050 reversal** is a modeled consequence of actual awards and payers, not a valuation of missed work. A fixed 5,000-minute prospective overtime block also restores all service, but costs **$140,806.45** across the window; its $15,000 overtime outlay is already inside that figure. The declared whole-market offer policy buys some capacity that awards leave unused. It is not optimized fallback procurement.

The digital-improved human-only comparison completes the identical obligations for **$87,300**, versus **$127,197.74** in the fully supported robot/staff-cut control. Ending robot assets, receivables, claims and unmodeled lifecycle costs remain; the window difference is not ROI, payback or a demonstrated employment effect. A model that only values removed regular staffing would miss both the conventional alternative and the switching/fallback costs.

### A received spare is not timely qualified service

A named actuator replacement is due in November (month 4). In one case its compatible spare is physically present but does not qualify until January's phase 4. Monthly support is admitted earlier, in phase 3; repair therefore completes in February (month 7). The successful September still triggered binding December notice. Retained human capacity covers November, but December and January each miss 50 cleaning zones. The final total is **3,900 zones**, all 160 inventory jobs, and six complete months. Qualification does not backfill earlier service, and failed support consumes neither the spare nor annual minutes.

If the manufacturing board never qualifies, no robot is manufactured and no staffing notice is issued. The fully retained human crew completes service. Consigned unissued inventory is not charged an invented supplier-financing cost: the current model prices parts once inside paid manufacturing/support bundles. This assumption remains a real commercial evidence gap.

Giving both operating companies the same robot access does not double the shared provider pool. Two robots compete under the existing deterministic admission order for the same 2,600 first-year support minutes; four bookings fail. Separate site crews and legal cash remain distinct even after common equity ownership. More procured robots therefore do not guarantee more complete service. This is an allocation-policy witness, not an optimized fleet allocation or a measured exclusive technology advantage.

### Target decision and broader scope

Before calling a paid block removable, match its notice horizon to a dated forward plan: qualified parts and replacement timing, provider entitlement and allocated capacity, retained site duties, funded fallback, actual customer contract commitments and each payer. A successful prior month is necessary under this declared rule but insufficient for future coverage. A review must also preserve the option to retain staffing, defer procurement or improve the human workflow.

The three decision packs carry this forward-coverage gate without transferring cleaning minutes, prices or staffing blocks. Hospital delivery requires timely repair coverage and a complete qualified handling/fallback roster; automotive workcells require compatible parts, line qualification and retained technician work; utility inspection requires accepted information, the intervention it changes, site work, analyst duties and support rights. Construction installation additionally needs trade/permit competence; warehouse tote service needs safe handling, handoffs and residual inventory duties. None has an empirical payroll coefficient supplied by this fixture. Substantial H contributes dated coordination and a better human baseline; I/J/K retain embodiment, qualified supply and model/support rights rather than treating software capability as support entitlement.

The source access limits from edition 0.43 remain closed. No new public-source claim or numerical calibration was introduced. Current operator accepted-service/maintenance decisions, actual paid work and all-payer costs remain missing; this integration resolves a model interface, not that empirical dependency.

## Intelligence roles, staffing payers and actual awards, edition 0.45

[The architecture/award execution](../model/results/architecture_awards.json) brings the four existing intelligence arrangements into the same entry, customer-award, qualified-resource and paid-crew history. Each complete case configures one target service business and a separate human-led rival. The target's customer company, physical service operator, OEM, model supplier and integrator have explicit roles; roles may share a legal account only where the selected arrangement says so. This is one physical loop and Treasury, not a sum of the earlier architecture and competition results. The default hybrid keeps separate declared operating-company offers after common ownership; it is not independent competition or optimized group pricing.

### The employer, asset owner and customer-contract owner can differ

In the model-led operator arrangement the customer company retains its end-market contracts, service receipts and ultimate-equity register. The separately contracted model operator employs the target's paid crew, pays its staffing, overtime, site/software and operating costs, and buys and owns its robot. The operator also occupies the model-supplier/integrator roles. Acquiring the customer company does not acquire that separate operator's robot, pool its cash or transfer its staff. The rival funds its own qualified human crew. Customer-contract cash rows and service-operator cash therefore refer to different legal accounts.

The target service contract pays a declared amount per accepted cleaning zone after delivery and includes the retained inventory duties. No separate inventory fee is assumed. Same-entity fees are eliminated; external model, integration and operator prices remain transfers within the modeled production boundary. Their actual payment can matter for liquidity without becoming another resource expense. All-payer resource/transaction cash independently equals the modeled outside resource suppliers' receipts, including the architecture resource supplier once. The customer company's paid labor and an outsourced operator's paid labor must not be confused with one another or with released task time.

### Equal capital can leave service unfunded

All nine displayed worlds have **$600,000 of declared logical-role opening capital**, and the same total cash across all modeled accounts. With $200,000 at the customer company, $200,000 at the integrated model operator and $200,000 at the hardware OEM, the model-operator case completes **4,000 cleaning zones and 160 inventory jobs**. It has six supported robot-months and eight complete service months.

Move the operator's $200,000 to the customer while keeping total capital unchanged. The operator cannot pay its initial mandatory crew and site obligations. Future service fees cannot fund earlier work that has not occurred, and an acquisition of the customer company creates no automatic operator funding transfer. The human rival completes **2,400 of 4,000 zones**, but all **160 inventory jobs remain undone**. The operator ends with **$40,400 of due unpaid obligations**, while the customer still holds its $400,000. The OEM has paid **$36,000** to manufacture a unit which remains its unsold asset. Cash availability in the production system is not cash availability at the party responsible for delivery.

The matched no-robot version fails in exactly the same way: 2,400 zones, zero inventory jobs and $40,400 of unpaid operator obligations. Its resource outlay is $36,000 lower because no robot is manufactured. That paired result identifies a funding/contract failure rather than a robot-capability effect. Lower cash spending alongside missing service and unpaid bills is not a valid saving.

When properly funded, both the licensed human-only and outsourced-operator human-only comparisons complete the identical work for **$87,300** of all-payer resource/transaction cash. The four fully funded robot arrangements complete the work for **$151,097.74** (integrated OEM), **$148,847.72** (licensed independent), **$170,197.72** (open adapted) and **$149,002.76** (model-led operator). These figures reflect catalog assumptions, paid manufacture, adaptation/qualification conventions, retained staffing, actual awards and ending assets/claims. They do not rank real architectures, estimate lab economics or establish lifecycle ROI.

## Paid development before service admission, edition 0.48

The existing first-successful-admission convention omitted paid attempts that failed before a robot could serve. The new [paid-development contract](../model/PAID_DEVELOPMENT.md) closes that bounded interface in the same architecture, paid-crew, qualified-parts, provider-resource and actual-award history. It charges eligible funded reviews when work occurs. A failed completed review remains paid; an unfunded or ineligible booking performs no work. The declared outcome gates admission without changing assumed capability or inventing a learning rate.

The first-pass case preserves the preceding successful case's $148,847.72 all-payer resource/transaction cash, 4,000 accepted cleaning zones and 160 inventory jobs. Its review resources are $19,500, already included in that total. One paid failure followed by a declared pass consumes $34,000 of review resources and $164,323.10 of total resource/transaction cash. It defers effective staffing notice from month 5 to month 6 and pays $44,000 of regular staffing instead of $42,000. These are synthetic conditional results; the difference includes changed service timing and recurring payments, not simply a new fee added to an unchanged world.

Stopping after the failed review retains $19,500 of paid work and a purchased robot awaiting commissioning. The human crews still complete the same service obligations, at $142,900 total resource/transaction cash. A matched human-only policy starts with no robot or development and completes those obligations at $87,300. This does not value the stranded hardware or claims, identify an optimal stopping rule, or establish a real robotics-versus-human cost ranking. The sunk payment belongs in historical cash; it does not make the next review necessary.

The continue-versus-stop comparison verifies that the two policies share every realized physical and cash event through month 2, including the paid failure. From month 3 onward, continuing consumes an additional **$21,423.10** of external resource/transaction cash while reducing regular staffing payments by **$4,000**. Both policies complete the same remaining **2,500 cleaning zones and 100 inventory jobs**. The comparison excludes earlier spending and reports the difference by legal payer. It does not declare stopping optimal: one world ends with installed hardware and the other with hardware awaiting commissioning, and their future benefits and asset values remain unknown. The separate human-only case is not substituted for this same-history comparison.

The support-delay case makes the new interface especially visible. A review passes and is paid in month 2, but the declared 2026 provider allocation cannot admit the robot. January capacity permits commissioning only in month 6. Review costs remain $19,500, supported robot-months fall to two, and the staffing notice would become effective in month 9, beyond this eight-month window. Buying a successful review therefore does not buy immediate service or paid-hours removal.

Rights and payer identity remain hard gates. With evidence-use rights unavailable there is no review, no review charge and no qualification; an already manufactured asset may still be stranded under the fixed purchase plan. At the exact engineering threshold, 120 hours permits the licensed review while 119 permits no review. Moving funds to another legal party does not finance the responsible supplier's work. Same-entity fees are eliminated, paid resources counted once, and neither a share acquisition nor a declared pass supplies another party's rights or staff.

The [AATIS and UKPN operator closeouts](quadruped_operator_followup.md#demonstration-approval-and-the-build-versus-buy-decision-edition-046) motivate the distinction between work, qualification, continuation and stopping. They do not supply these prices, hours, outcomes or policies. This fixture does not import automotive-kit learning rates, pool unrelated episodes, perform training or model detailed trial physics. A useful next empirical record would connect dated work packages and payer payments to actual qualification, accepted service and changed paid commitments. A future numerical composition needs an explicit compatible learning/version and qualified-alternative contract first.
