3. **Proposition on directed learning and who can pay.** Compare two initial deployments and one optional, physically indivisible follow-on service. For source $j$, let $k_a^j$ be payer $a$’s remaining qualification bill after permitted, validated transfer, $b_a^j$ its available balance before commitment, and $t$ the capacities of enforceable payment links. With divisible, costless payments, the following condition is necessary and sufficient for funding.

   `F_j = 1 iff k^j(U) ≤ b^j(U) + t(incoming U) for every payer subset U.`

   Let $m_j$ be the project’s current net return over a common starting endowment and $g_j$ the participants’ combined incremental net return from completing the follow-on over their fallback. Include the qualification bills once and require implementable participation terms. In the two-period additive benchmark the ranking is by `m_j + F_j max(0, g_j)`. If H earns more today and only L can fund the target, L wins exactly when `g_L > m_H − m_L`. When both can fund, H regains priority exactly if `m_H + max(0,g_H) > m_L + max(0,g_L)`. Equality gives a tie. The existing two-payer example crosses at a prepayment capacity of one. Its companion example relocates an equal validated saving across payers, holding physical service, demand and aggregate resources fixed. **Pass** when the paper states both conditions, exhibits the binding payer and shows when pooling, a paid equivalent contract or weak demand removes the ordering. Standard funding-cut arithmetic is the benchmark. [Two-period reversal](qualification_finance.md) · [Experience threshold and pooling comparison](learning_funding_alignment.md)

4. **Proposition on public qualification and the older procedure.** Hold current service, cost, rights, and the continuation conditional on each public outcome fixed across two procedures. Only the probability of qualification differs. Under expected additive payoffs the newer procedure's advantage is `(p_new − p_old)(V_qualified − V_unqualified)`. Derive both continuation values from a market for timely service. In the fixed-rival benchmark, retaining the old recipe makes the conditional gain weakly positive. The economic question is whether a public change in qualified capacity can reverse that sign with a unique rival response. [Optional-capability result](../../papers/integrated_firm/PROPOSITIONS.md) · [Funded action quotient](../../papers/integrated_firm/FUNDED_EMBODIED.md)

   A hand-checkable two-price benchmark makes this target concrete. Each firm must deliver every kit it quotes by the deadline. Prices are 2 or 4, orders are `7 − 2 × own price + rival price`, and unit costs are 0 and 1. The first firm's certified capacity is four kits on failure and eight on success. Its rival can always serve eight. Only the high quote is feasible on failure, so the rival also charges 4 and the first firm earns 12. On public success the low quote strictly dominates for the first firm. The rival then strictly prefers 2, and the first firm earns 10. Holding the rival at 4 would instead raise the first firm's profit to 14. Qualification preserves the old quote and changes only the first firm's physical service eligibility. These are illustrative units in a price game with two admissible quotes. At equal attempt cost, even a strictly increasing nonlinear utility ranks a higher probability of earning 10 instead of 12 adversely here. **Pass** when the strict best-response and capacity inequalities are characterized, including the boundary where they fail. Test enforceable commitment to the old offer, private certification and the option to skip either attempt. Either attempt could lose to abstention even when the two attempts rank strictly. Any extension to the integrated quantity game needs its own argument.
