# Who can verify a qualification, and who sees it before bidding?

A change of fixture, end effector or reset procedure can require a controller to be qualified again at the receiving site. Two institutional facts matter. Can a contracting party establish that the specified controller actually ran the target protocol? Can a competitor see the resulting capacity before it bids? An audit delivered privately to the customer can answer the first question while leaving the rival uninformed.

## 1. When an advance can finance validation and still fail to induce it

Give the parties the strongest simple contract first. When contributions and qualification can be enforced and the supplier has spare cash, it can advance one unit before commissioning. Moving that unit from its adaptation bill to the operator's account reproduces both parties' outlays under the alternative learning destination. Technical uncertainty alone need not prevent an advance. The original example with five units of captured operating profit per party supports the pooling contract. [Funding cuts](qualification_finance.md) · [Pooling and pledgeability](contracting_and_pledgeability.md)

Consider a different, explicit restriction. Only the target operator may use the local recovery traces and authorize a controller version on its cell. The operator can meet the same shipment deadline using its established fallback. A paid invoice or a delivered kit looks the same whichever method produced it. The operator controls the validation resources and can retain or redirect them. Absent an audit right, neither their use on that controller nor the resulting internal saving can be verified for settlement. There is one contracting round. Each party keeps its own incremental operating gain over fallback. If a target test is admissible for settlement, the contract can also pay a bonus for passing it. In the example below, validation produces a pass for certain, fallback cannot produce one, and verification has zero incremental cost.

Write $k_O$ for the operator's avoidable validation cost and $v_O$ for its private gain if it validates. An unrestricted advance $a$ enters its cash in either branch. Let $d$ be the difference in expected *enforceable* transfers between validation and fallback. Once funded, the operator validates strictly when $v_O+d>k_O$, with indifference at equality. If the two actions produce the same distribution of all admissible records, every contract on those records has $d=0$. For a saving of size $s$, the two learning destinations straddle the incentive threshold whenever $k_O-s<v_O<k_O$. If the operator has enough cash for the reduced bill only, and supplier participation holds, a saving on the operator bill makes implementation possible. An advance against the original bill covers the cash shortfall yet leaves the incentive unchanged. Assignable target receipts, an auditor with access to the versioned target log, or a supplier able to buy the target test directly can change that conclusion.

**Equal-saving comparison.** Supplier and operator start with cash $(5,1)$. Before learning their required outlays are $(2,2)$. Either source saves exactly one unit on the same follow-on qualification. Set incremental private gains on actual qualification to $(4,1.5)$, with zero incremental gains under fallback. Both sources would create the same net project surplus of $4+1.5-3=2.5$ if implemented.

| Where the saving lands | Bills, supplier / operator | Implementation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Operator commissioning | 2 / 1 | The operator can pay 1 and earns 1.5 from validating. The supplier earns 4 against its bill of 2. |
| Supplier adaptation | 1 / 2 | An advance of 1 covers the cash gap. With identical admissible records the operator can keep the advance and avoid spending 2 for a private gain of 1.5. A larger unconditional payment leaves the same incentive. |
| Supplier adaptation with an auditable target test | 1 / 2 | Advance 1 and promise 0.75 on passing the target test. Validation then gains the operator $1.5+0.75-2=0.25$ over diversion. Supplier participation yields $4-1-1-0.75=1.25$. |

If the operator privately gains more than 2 from validating, an unconditional advance can work even with a private log, provided the supplier will fund it. The funding calculation is standard, as is the hidden-action incentive constraint. The deployment-specific prediction is that granting **audit access to the target configuration** can erase a learning-destination effect at identical physical capability, liquidity and total validated saving. Access to the source log alone cannot distinguish the two target actions in this example. An escrow or acceptance payment that can distinguish them is a counterexample to applying the friction. The automotive fixture's $336 is the customer's additional outlay for its matched service window. Interpreting that number as a qualification bill, a required advance, or an incentive payment would require evidence the replay lacks. ## 2. Public capacity and private qualification in the price example

The milestone example has two simultaneous quotes per firm, 4 and 2. A firm must be qualified to fill every order its quote can attract. Its orders are `7 − 2 × own quote + rival quote`. Unit costs are 0 and 1. The first firm can serve four kits on failure and eight on success. The rival can always serve eight. Private operating profits in synthetic units are below. [Milestone ledger](milestones.md)

| First firm's quote | Rival quotes 4 | Rival quotes 2 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 4, always feasible | (12, 9) | (4, 7) |
| 2, requires qualification | (14, 3) | (10, 5) |

With public capacity, failure yields (4,4) in quotes. After success the first firm strictly prefers 2 against either quote, and the rival's unique best reply is 2. Holding the rival at 4 gives the first firm 14 after success. Allowing the reply gives it 10, down from 12 on failure. The advantage of the higher-success procedure at equal cost is therefore $-2(p_H-p_L)$. This ranks attempts against one another. Abstention remains a separate alternative.

With a **private outcome**, suppose the rival knows the probability $p$ of success. Before the simultaneous quotes it earns $9-6p$ from quoting 4 and $7-2p$ from quoting 2. It quotes 4 below $p=1/2$ and 2 above. At equality either quote or any mixture is optimal. At a common rival belief, realized success raises the first firm's payoff by 2 against the high quote and by 6 against the low quote. If both the procedure choice and outcome are hidden and there is no other signal, a unilateral change of procedure leaves the rival's information unchanged. The higher-success procedure is strictly preferable between equal-cost attempts.

If the **procedure is credibly disclosed** while its outcome stays private, the rival can price against the disclosed odds. The choice is observable or binding before bids in this case. The first firm's ex-ante profit is $12+2p$ below the threshold and $4+6p$ above it. Switching from $p=1/4$ to $p=3/4$ cuts expected profit from 12.5 to 8.5. Thus a rival need not see the actual release to penalize a more ambitious *announced* method. A field test needs the tender's disclosure rules and timestamps. A third-party safety certificate contributes to this mechanism only insofar as competitors can see it before quoting and infer the relevant service capacity from it. A confidential report can still support an enforceable customer payment.

## 3. The matched quantity check

First keep the price example's demand, costs and capacity numbers. Let firms commit continuous quantities and let prices clear, which also removes the two-quote restriction. Inverting demand gives $p_i=7-(2q_i+q_j)/3$. The best responses at the positive solutions are `q₁ = min(K₁, (21 − q₂)/4)` and `q₂ = min(8, (18 − q₁)/4)`. With capacity four, the unique quantities are $(4,7/2)$ and the first firm's profit is $38/3$. With capacity eight they are $(22/5,17/5)$ and profit is $968/75$. Qualification raises profit by $6/25$. The rival cuts its quantity slightly as the first firm expands.

In a one-market quantity game with downward-sloping demand, suppose qualification preserves the first firm's old quantity and cost and the rival weakly reduces output in response. The old quantity then earns at least its former profit. Optimizing against the new rival quantity can only improve on that. The integrated game has two markets, shared capacity and several dates, so the sign of the rival's response needs to be checked there.

The diagnostic reads payoff matrices from the archived four-date embodied game at $B=120$, procedure cap 2, with the original resource constraints, recipes, prices, procurement and continuation rules. At each reachable decision state, including off-path states, it compares adjacent retained success probabilities for the same local physical or digital qualification attempt wherever both are legal. It checks equality of every other action field, fixes the rival's simultaneous action, and allows both firms to respond to the subsequent public outcome. Each difference includes those future equilibrium responses. Both firms, all retained rival actions and both published selectors enter. The two full replays match the archived results, including dated cash and deviations.
