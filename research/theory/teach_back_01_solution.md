# Teach-back 1 — separate solution

1. H without expansion yields `8+(4/5)*3=52/5`. Expanding H reduces value by `3-(4/5)*(4-3)=11/5`. L without expansion yields `2+(4/5)*9=46/5`. L expansion would add `13/5`, but cash 2 cannot pay cost 3. H wins without credit.
2. L borrows 1, adds its cash 2 and pays 3. Future operating profit is `(4/5)*16=64/5`; repayment leaves `59/5`, above H's `52/5`. Principal appears as +1 then -1, and creates no lifetime resources. It changes timing feasibility. For L, q=2, revenue=20, resource cost=4, service fee=84/5, customer profit=16/5. Customer/provider operating profits sum to 16. Financing and investment costs remain separate. The lender receives the principal with zero interest in this illustration.
3. L's funded value is `2-3+(4/5)*12=43/5`; H wins. Both access and learning matter in this parameterization. This does not prove both are necessary in every deployment problem.
4. Differentiate the log: `phi/(1+m)^2+(y-1)/(1+m)`. Its zero gives the stated m*, when y<1 and the solution is positive and admissible. Competence multiplies productivity and cancels out of this derivative. Qualified capacity, demand saturation, unit costs and integer constraints can change the economic fleet decision.
5. A fixed target task/configuration with matched starting policy and adaptation budget, randomized or credibly matched inclusion of eligible source data, and held-out accepted-service evaluation can isolate a transfer effect. Demand growth, easier selected sites, simultaneous hardware upgrades and staff learning confound uncontrolled before/after results. Joining that evaluation to dated collections/obligations tests financeability; it does not make customer demand exogenous.

The most damaging prior-art objection is that the deployment reversal is a standard constrained-investment result. A paper needs a specific additional consequence of transfer, qualification, payment structure or a rigorous negative finding. Naming these states after robots is not enough.
