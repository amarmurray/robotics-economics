# Entry changes ownership before it changes production

A platform can buy an existing service business without creating a new customer, robot or accepted unit of output. That distinction now executes in the [funded entry-transition model](../model/ENTRY_TRANSITIONS.md), using the existing monthly cleaning-zone, cohort, customer and Treasury loop. Fifteen complete worlds add bounded **DE-CF06 acquisition without new demand** and **DE-CF07 hybrid transition**. They are invented contract sensitivities, not empirical company valuations or a recommendation to transact.

## Purchase price does not capitalize the operating company

The reference target serves 500 recurring synthetic zones per month with two existing leased robots and retained paid staffing. Status quo and a funded share acquisition both deliver 4,000 zones over eight months. They retain exactly the same physical events, units, service ledger and target operating cash. Increasing the hypothetical share price from $100,000 to $150,000 moves another $50,000 from buyer to seller without changing production or consolidated resource outlays. It leaves the buyer less liquid. No terminal equity value is supplied, so neither the cash table nor the output table ranks the investments.

Share ownership changes in month two only after the actual closing group settles. The lessor still owns the robots; the target company remains their custodian and operating counterparty. Its pre-close receivables still pay that company, and an existing $30,000 payable is not erased or reassigned to the seller. In the liability stress case, the funded acquisition closes while overdue obligations halt target service. A positive target bank balance does not establish readiness when a full required invoice cannot settle.

With zero target opening cash, the $100,000 price can still reach the seller while the target delivers zero service. A separate $25,000 working-capital injection at month two restores 3,000 later zones, not the 1,000 missed in the first two months. Consideration and operating capitalization solve different constraints. The injection must itself be financed by the buyer; no target cash, paid customer invoice or hypothetical share value is available to pay the purchase price automatically.

## Closing is a funded event with retained failed-deal costs

The reference pays $1,000 for prior diligence and $5,000 for conditional transition resources. Buyer opening cash of $106,000 exactly funds those bills plus the $100,000 consideration; one cent less blocks the whole contingent closing. No partial seller payment, transition payment, target injection or ownership change survives a failed candidate settlement. The already incurred diligence remains. Missing assumed transfer consent also blocks closing while retaining diligence cost. These are declared synthetic terms, not statements of legal priority or market deal practice.

A one-month assumed service interruption after successful closing loses 500 zones. The target's $32,000 eight-month paid staffing commitment and physical unit identities remain. Smaller operating cash outlays during an interruption can reflect work that was never supported, not improved efficiency. The model does not invent fallback coverage or recover the missed service later.

## Hybrid adds an operating leg rather than duplicating the base

The four route comparisons each begin with $200,000 of buyer cash. Organic build commits $60,000 to a separate company, obtains robots through actual funded supply/installation/support and schedules customer moves. It captures 70 baseline zones and assigns 150 unmet/new zones that were already in the market ledger. It delivers 4,750 total zones over the window. The seller-owned incumbent loses only the explicitly reassigned customers; its existing staffing and assets remain.

Hybrid pays for the target and separately funds the new company. The acquired 500-zone baseline stays assigned to the target; only 150 existing unmet/new zones move to the growth company. The standard hybrid delivers 4,717 zones. Its narrower growth-company sales leave a late liquidity shortfall even while cash remains at the parent. Moving another $5,000 of the same opening parent capital into that company restores 4,750 zones without adding robots or demand. This is a cash-allocation sensitivity within a fixed policy, not an optimal capitalization rule.

The independently committed growth leg can proceed even when the share closing fails. That adverse world explicitly remains an organic growth position with a failed acquisition, rather than being relabeled a successful hybrid platform. Acquisition itself earns no automatic productivity, staffing or data-learning bonus. The reference also retains fixed prompt/delayed incumbent responses: a funded return of 70 baseline customers changes assignment and collections, not aggregate demand. Competing onboarding costs and cash pressure can change total delivered service. These cases do not calculate acquisition payback, prove a durable entry advantage or optimize the incumbent's response.

## Implication for the decision packs and thesis T3

For hospital, utility and automotive entry, separate three questions: whether the existing company owns or controls the relevant service relationships; whether its operating obligations and technical rights survive the proposed transaction; and whether actual funds can support the desired new physical process. The cleaning-zone cases provide conservation and failure mechanisms only. They do not import their prices, durations, costs, customer-transfer ability or approvals into another service.

Thesis T3 is strengthened: equal technology access and aggregate capital leave customer assignment, legal accounts, working capital and service continuity unresolved. Acquisition can buy control of existing activity; growth still requires additional funded capacity and customers within a conserved market. The strongest counterargument remains that a real platform could improve processes or capital allocation materially. Evidence of those improvements must be measured against inherited obligations and the incumbent's feasible response, not supplied by an acquisition label.

The next work shifts to the unresolved service-specific alternatives and qualification evidence, starting with utility residual visits/fixed sensing and the automotive workcell comparator. Real all-actor costs, retained paid work, qualified supply, operating rights, support and endogenous learning remain incomplete. Asset purchase/redeployment, legal restructuring, valuation and broad market equilibrium are outside this bounded share-transfer implementation. The active 168-hour campaign clock is unchanged.

## Actual renewal and company entry, edition 0.38

The initial entry model preserved legal entities and funded closing, but customer movements were scheduled scenario inputs. The [joint execution](../model/results/renewing_entry.json) now subjects status quo, standalone build, share acquisition and hybrid formation to the existing funded renewal rule inside one physical and Treasury run. Ten worlds deepen DE-CF06/07 and the renewal challenges; fourteen families contain 215 bounded worlds. All earlier numerical cases remain unchanged.

A lot represents a recurring service contract with an identified operator, locked price and expiry. Awards require currently funded capacity and paid onboarding/switching work; later renewal may respond to actual prior service. The equity transaction occurs in phase two, before physical work and phase-five renewal. It changes ultimate ownership, while target cash, asset title, receivables, inherited bills and existing contract terms remain with the same legal company. The purchase uses the parent's available cash after any separately committed growth funding and prior diligence. It does not borrow the target's bank balance.

**Buying contracts is not winning them through service.** The acquisition-only world reclassifies 500 zones of recurring contract volume at closing, gains no new contract lots and delivers the same 4,000 zones as status quo. Share consideration is $100,000 to the seller, and diligence/transition resources are $6,000. That changes ownership and cash, not production. Terminal equity value and returns remain unknown.

The standalone build first wins 100 zones of recurring lot volume, but ends with no assigned lots after the declared incumbent response. Equal access to supported robots does not preserve the entrant's initial price advantage under these offer rules. The hybrid world first wins 100 zones of recurring lot volume through the separate operating entrant. At closing, 400 zones of the target's existing contract volume are reclassified to the platform. A later 100-zone transfer between commonly owned operating firms is internal reassignment, not another external customer win. Both firms continue separately declared offer mandates after common ownership. This explicit organizational premise is not independent competition, optimal group pricing or a claimed real market outcome.

A one-month target interruption in the hybrid case reduces that month's delivery from 500 to 200 zones. Later service returns to 500, but the 300 missed zones remain lost. With three-month locked contracts and no breach exit, the same interruption leaves only 100 zones delivered in that month and 400 permanently missed. Both settings have seven complete months. Gross later awards from unassigned contracts include recovered work; new-platform contract-lot IDs count first awards only and exclude earlier platform ownership or equity reclassification. Neither measure counts unique people or inferred customer acquisition.

**Complete market service can coexist with an unfunded acquired company.** An inherited $30,000 target bill remains due to the original counterparty and is eventually paid. In the uninjected case, other target operating obligations then accumulate to $15,100 due and unpaid by the final month, while the entrant takes more internal work and the overall market still receives all 4,000 zones. A separately funded $30,000 target equity injection avoids those closing arrears in the matched case, leaving the parent only $4,000. The acquisition price itself never supplied that operating money. Named-bill settlement is therefore insufficient evidence that the company is fully funded.

The parent also faces a binding closing boundary. With $60,000 committed to growth, $1,000 diligence, $100,000 consideration and $5,000 transition resources, $166,000 of parent opening funds closes; $165,999.99 does not. Failed closing retains diligence and the separately funded growth leg, pays no partial consideration/transition/equity group and changes no target ownership. In the saved $160,000 case, growth continues, the acquisition fails and the parent closes with $99,000. No closing retry or automatic cash sweep is invented.

The stronger human/competitive-response case keeps the same paid staff and software charge, assumes higher digital-human efficiency and no new robots, and uses the declared incumbent response offer. It delivers all 4,000 zones while the entrant wins no new lots. Its bounded external operating/transaction cash is $71,700, versus $253,408.38 for standalone robotic build and $259,408.38 for the base hybrid. These remain conditional window cash comparisons, with different retained assets and no terminal valuation, empirical uplift or lifecycle ROI.

For hospital, utility and automotive packs, acquisition and operating-contract eligibility are separate gates. Verify actual control/consent, inherited obligations, funding after transaction costs, service interruption, renewal or breach rights and the competent incumbent alternative before interpreting acquired revenue as entry advantage. The numerical cleaning prices, contracts, staff and disruption assumptions do not transfer. The joint model still lacks the shared crew, annual/typed-part constraints, four-architecture roles, asset recovery and endogenous learning from other modules; it does not establish a simultaneously feasible six-cell portfolio.

## Funded recovery and actual retention, edition 0.39

The [combined recovery execution](../model/results/recovering_entry.json) joins company entry, real scheduled payments, robot ownership, debt default, relocation, qualification and actual customer renewal in one physical/Treasury history. It adds thirteen bounded challenges inside the existing families, bringing their total to228. The common-owner firms retain separate declared offer mandates and legal bank accounts. This is neither optimal restructuring nor an empirical estimate of acquisition/recovery value.

**The robot has a paid history.** The standard case opens with no installed or finished robot. The provider spends $36,000 manufacturing one identified unit. An outside lender actually advances $60,000 to the target; the target later pays the provider $60,000 for the robot, which ships in month1 and is commissioned in month2. The parent separately buys the target's shares in month2. A subsequent asset sale names that same unit, a distinct operating-company buyer, actual title, current default, explicit release/configuration rights, $30,000 consideration, $1,000 relocation and two months to arrival. Neither share ownership nor a loan claim grants a right to sell a lessor's robot. The lease comparison has different actual payment flows and no target debt default here; it is a title-refusal control, not a matched default experiment.

**Cash cure can restore service before physical reuse.** With no sale, the target defaults in month4 and the eight-month market receives2,400 of4,000 zones, with $20,732.58 principal still owed. With the funded $30,000 sale, the target remains in default in the sale month because debt servicing already occurred. Next month's scheduled settlement pays its remaining debt and restores its human operation. The whole market returns to500 zones in month5; the relocated robot first qualifies and receives support in month6. The window totals3,600 zones: the400 missed in month4 are never restored. Sale price is an internal transfer, while principal payment actually returns funds to the outside lender.

The destination may never qualify the robot, or it may arrive after the observation window. Both cases retain the paid sale and relocation, leave a buyer-owned stranded unit, repay the target debt and still deliver the same3,600 total zones through the recovered human operation. Therefore the whole-market service recovery in this fixture cannot be attributed to successful robot reuse. Actual operator allocation and expenditures differ: all-payer resource/transaction cash is $137,315.16 with successful qualification and $127,582.58 with no qualification. Those are conditional window outlays with different retained assets, not lifecycle cost or returns.

A lower $20,000 sale price permits physical reuse in month6 but leaves the target in default with $5,782.58 of principal outstanding. Only2,850 zones are delivered. Thus successful robot relocation does not itself cure the seller or preserve the complete service obligation. With locked three-month customer terms and no breach exit, the $30,000 transfer case loses500 zones in its default month rather than400; later cash and service recovery leave that shortfall intact.

**The purchase boundary is not an operating-funding boundary.** $46,050 of initial growth-company equity leaves exactly $31,000 available at the month4 offer, enough for purchase plus initial relocation. One cent less refuses both contingent payments and leaves title unchanged. The exact-funded purchase nonetheless empties the buyer's operating funds, leaves the robot uncommissioned at the horizon and delivers only3,400 zones. This is the same distinction highlighted by the earlier fallback-reserve investigation: funds committed to an asset can remove the cash needed for service. The separate workforce/resource execution has not been silently joined here.

A $30,000 parent-to-target equity injection at the earlier share closing prevents target default, so the recovery policy refuses an unnecessary owner sale. All4,000 zones are served; the parent retains only $4,000. The operating companies, parent and financier face different cash positions from the asset-sale route. This declared prior action is not an optimized funding policy, an automatic group cash pool or evidence of a real financing offer.

The competent human-buyer pair pays the entrant the same $4,000 monthly staff budget as the incumbent and recomputes capacity from the existing wage/task/calendar mapping. With or without the later robot purchase it serves all4,000 zones. Buying the robot raises all-payer resource/transaction outlay from $146,632.58 to $158,565.16 without adding accepted service, while returning more actual cash to the lender and reducing its remaining principal claim. Creditor recovery and service productivity are different outcomes. The digital-human mapping and fixed firm offers remain synthetic; no observed staffing removal or digital uplift is inferred.

The all-payer figures above add outside customer switching resources to the existing fixed operator/provider/parent/seller boundary. They count manufacture, paid staffing/software and transaction/relocation/qualification resources once. Internal hardware and later asset prices, equity and loan principal remain separately traced transfers; outstanding operating bills, interest, credit claims and physical assets are retained, not converted to profit or terminal value.

For hospital logistics, utility inspection and automotive presentation, verify ownership and configuration/support rights separately from company control; reconstruct actual original asset funding and every payer; and test retained service while sale, debt cure, relocation and requalification occur at different times. Software/model access, customer acceptance, residual physical duties and provider support belong in those gates. The same method challenges cleaning, tote handoff and construction; no cleaning price, staffing ratio or deadline transfers to those services. Shared crew, annual resources, typed parts, four intelligence architectures, endogenous learning and a simultaneously feasible six-cell portfolio remain separately executed or incomplete. Actual matched full-service costs, labor transitions, rights and residual values remain empirical gaps.
